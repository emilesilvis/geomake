# Evidence for a daily geometry puzzle ladder

Research checked 6 September 2026. The intended players are two adult family members. The recommendation is to **curate a progression of geometric discoveries, use the generator for reliable variations, and calibrate the experience with the two actual players**. The sources below support parts of this approach; none establishes a formula for a satisfying daily family ritual.

The starting repository (`5b88f55`) contained backwards generation, 18 recipes, and a lexicographic ladder score built from depth, solution length, width, shape type, and construction operations. Those are useful content descriptors. Ordering them does not itself measure a person's difficulty or enjoyment. The [README](../README.md) now also documents the seven-day pilot developed during this investigation.

## Established approaches we can reuse

| Approach | What the primary source actually establishes | Proposed use here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **GeomVerse: backwards generation and reasoning structure** | The authors define depth as reasoning hops and width as maximum branching in a proof, then generate problems backwards by replacing a needed value with another derivation. They compare models and include a small human check: four educated participants solved a total of 20 problems per depth. Their limitations explicitly say the problems may require little creativity. [Paper, §§3, 4.1 and limitations](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.12241) | Keep reproducible generation and structural metadata. Add authored concept/prerequisite information and player feedback. The study does **not** validate this repo's composite ordering or show that adding steps produces a better puzzle. |
| **Euclidea: fundamentals, exploration, elegance** | The creator's site describes progression from basic constructions to harder ones, hints, automatic solution verification, draggable constructions, and higher scores for fewer moves. Learned constructions become shortcut tools. [Official Euclidea site](https://www.euclidea.xyz/) | Let earlier insights become reusable tools in later problems. Distinguish reaching an answer from an optional challenge to find a cleaner explanation. Construction-game move counts are not an appropriate universal score for shaded-area reasoning. |
| **Mathigon: concept relationships and guided exploration** | Mathigon describes building a knowledge graph of related concepts to identify missing prerequisites, together with interactive exploration and tailored hints. These are the maker's design description, not evidence that its adaptive model will transfer to our players. [Mathigon's approach](https://mathigon.org/about) | Begin with a small, explicit prerequisite graph; a full adaptive tutoring system is unnecessary for two people. Offer authored help when requested. Do not infer mastery solely from entering the correct number. |
| **NRICH: accessible starts, substantial extensions, discussion** | NRICH distinguishes mathematical prerequisites from the psychological difficulty of finding a first step. Its low-threshold/high-ceiling tasks offer accessible entry and harder extensions. Separate collaboration guidance recommends time to think individually, discussing with a partner, valuing both contributions, and asking how and why. [Accessible tasks](https://nrich.maths.org/articles/low-threshold-high-ceiling-introduction), [collaboration guidance](https://nrich.maths.org/articles/supporting-students-work-collaboratively) | Give both players the same main puzzle with optional support and extensions. Invite each to notice something before comparing methods. This is classroom practice guidance; applying it to an adult pair is a design hypothesis. |
| **Interleaving and spacing: revisit a method after other work** | In a study with 126 seventh-grade students, rearranging graph/slope practice improved later test performance; at 30 days, mean scores were 74% versus 42%. Novel test questions retained the practice format. The authors caution that greater transfer might reduce the benefit, and they did not survey student liking. [Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015), methods/results/discussion](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED557355.pdf) | Introduce an idea coherently, then revisit it later among other ideas so the solver must select a method. This supports testing delayed revisits, not promising adult enjoyment, distant transfer, or a particular optimal interval. |
| **GeoGebra: an optional interactive diagram engine** | Official documentation supports embedding the geometry app with `GGBApplet`, loading an activity, and communicating through an API with object creation and update listeners. [Embedding](https://geogebra.github.io/docs/reference/en/GeoGebra_Apps_Embedding/), [API](https://geogebra.github.io/docs/reference/en/GeoGebra_Apps_API/) | Consider it when dragging a constrained point, drawing auxiliary lines, or testing an invariant becomes central. It can provide the interaction layer; it does not supply the daily content sequence, spoiler policy, or the pair's history. No integration has been implemented or tested in this research task. |

The strongest direct support for **conceptual progression** is Euclidea's fundamentals-to-constructions sequence and Mathigon's prerequisite model. For **help alongside discovery**, Mathigon and Euclidea supply concrete product precedents, while the exact hint policy remains ours to test. For **variety and later reuse**, the interleaving study supplies learning evidence with explicit transfer limits. For **collaboration**, NRICH is the closest practical framework. None of these sources establishes that removing speed competition improves enjoyment for this particular adult pair; that should be a product choice they can revise.

## Design hypotheses for the first playable run

1. **Sequence insights, not just formulas.** Give each curated rung an intended realization, prerequisites, a reason it belongs after the previous rung, and a later use. For example: recognize a whole area; remove a known part; discover a useful auxiliary line; reuse the same decomposition in a different-looking figure; combine it with an earlier insight. Increased arithmetic is not sufficient evidence of increased insight.
2. **Give each day a satisfying stopping point.** Start with one common main puzzle, with an easier entry prompt and a harder extension available. Treat the extension as extra exploration. Preserve an unfinished puzzle and allow a return later. The right daily quantity and duration are unknown until the pair tries it.
3. **Make help deliberate and graduated.** Keep a nudge, a strategy hint, a concrete next step, and the full explanation separately revealable. The first hint should focus attention without stating the intended realization. A title, concept label, thumbnail, filename, or next-day preview can also spoil a puzzle and deserves review. Source products establish that hints can coexist with exploration; this four-stage policy is our hypothesis.
4. **Make sharing about reasoning.** Allow private thinking before either sees the other's answer, then offer a prompt such as “What made it click?” or “Can you explain it another way?” Default to no timer, public ranking, or punishment for using hints. Record a shared discussion separately from either person's own solve. These choices serve the stated family ritual; they are not conclusions of the school study.
5. **Make variation change the thinking occasionally.** New lengths in an otherwise identical recipe are replay, not evidence of transfer. Delayed revisits can change which quantity is given, rotate or recompose a figure, ask for a justification, or combine a familiar move with another idea. Hand-review these changes: they can accidentally obscure a necessary fact or introduce an unintended shortcut.

## What would validate the direction

Before calling the run ready, inspect every player-visible puzzle and its optional hints. Confirm the visible givens determine the target, the intended solution uses those givens, the diagram is readable, and the hint ordering preserves a meaningful discovery. A numerical answer check is useful evidence about the generated figure, but cannot alone establish those player-facing properties.

Then use a short actual playtest with both people. Record the puzzle/version, independent or shared solve, help revealed, whether it felt familiar, whether there was a satisfying realization, and whether they wanted to continue. Ask about enjoyment separately from effort and correctness. Adjust concept order and content from these observations before adding a complex adaptive scheduler. Two players provide direct evidence for their own preferences, not a population-level difficulty scale.
